# 14. The company view: a CEO review, and what an investor will ask

Written 15 November 2026, at your request. Two parts. First, a review of where Phew stands, as a CEO would read it. Second, what a venture capital firm reviews, where Phew is on each point today, and the plan to fill each one after the MVP (roadmap L17).

This is a view to argue with, not a decision. Numbers come from `11-direction.md` and `12-validation.md`; nothing here is new research.

## 14.1 Where Phew stands

**What is real.** A working Mac companion, built in five days of work: it reads the screen when called, explains, guides by pointing and follows your clicks, remembers your projects in an encrypted memory, continues as a conversation, and knows itself. 217 automatic tests. A question costs about 1.7 cents.

**What is decided but not built.** He acts in your apps by himself. He researches alone. He imports what other AI tools know. Voice. A finished look and feel.

**What is missing, in order of weight.**

1. **No user but you.** Not one person outside has used it or been asked. Every claim about demand is desk research, and that research says payment and staying are unproven.
2. **No reason yet why a big company cannot do it.** Apple, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Microsoft each ship screen-aware assistants, memory, and agents that act. Phew's pieces exist elsewhere one by one. The bet is the combination and the feel.
3. **No way to reach people.** Not signed, not distributable, needs the user's own API key.
4. **No business model tested.** No price, no payer found.
5. **One person, part time, with an AI doing the engineering.** That is a strength for speed and a question for an investor.

## 14.2 The sharpest version of the idea

Today's wording: "an AI collaboration companion for people who create projects". It is true and still wide. An investor will ask "for whom, exactly, and to do what, first".

A sharper wording to test:

> **Phew is the assistant that works inside the apps you already use. You say what you want; he does it there, in front of you, and remembers the project.**

Why this is the strong part:

- General assistants live in their own window or a browser. Agents take a task away and come back later. **Nobody owns "works with me, in my app, right now".** That is the gap the research found.
- It turns three built things (sees the screen, points, remembers) and one planned thing (acts) into one sentence.
- It gives a clear first user: **people who make things on a Mac across several apps and do not want to learn each one deeply**: founders, freelancers, designers who are not Excel people, analysts who are not Figma people.

What to stop saying: "learn software" as the headline, "privacy" as the headline (it is expected, not a reason to buy), and "Jarvis" outside your own notes (every assistant claims it).

## 14.3 A growth path that fits one founder

Four steps. Each has one question it must answer before the next begins.

| Step | What | The one question | Evidence that answers it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. MVP (now) | Finish acting, look and feel, agents. Use it daily yourself | Does it do real jobs right, in your apps? | 4 of 5 real jobs done right (roadmap PM.3) |
| 2. Ten users | Give it by hand to 10 people like you. Watch them use it | Do they come back in week 2 without being asked? | 5 of 10 still use it weekly after a month |
| 3. First money | A waiting list, a price, and a signed app people can install | Will a stranger pay? | 20 paying users, or the honest answer that they will not |
| 4. Raise or not | With steps 2 and 3 in hand, decide: stay a small paid tool, or raise to go wide | Is this a company or a product? | The numbers of step 3 |

What step 2 needs that the MVP does not have: an app others can install (signing, a hosted key or a simple way to bring a key), and a way to see whether they use it (counts only, with their consent).

The two honest ends of this path. If people use it and pay: a company, with the business version (software training for firms) as a second product. If they use it and do not pay: a well-made open tool and a strong portfolio piece. Both are real outcomes; step 2 tells them apart for the price of a few weeks.

## 14.4 What a venture capital firm reviews

Firms differ, but an early-stage review covers the same ground. Each row is a question they ask, what a good answer looks like, where Phew is today, and what L17 must produce.

| Area | The question | A good answer has | Phew today | To produce after the MVP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Problem** | Who hurts, how much, how often? | A named person, a painful moment, proof they already spend time or money on it | A reasoned problem, no interviews | 20 interviews; the 5 jobs people most want done for them |
| **Customer (ICP)** | Who buys first, exactly? | One narrow group you can list and reach | "People who create projects on a Mac": too wide | One first group, with where to find 100 of them |
| **Product** | What is it, and does it work? | A demo that makes the point in 60 seconds | Works for explain, guide, memory. Acting not built | A 60-second demo of him doing a real job across two apps |
| **Why now** | Why could this not exist 2 years ago? | A change in technology or behaviour | Models can now read screens and act reliably; people now accept AI at work | One paragraph with dated facts |
| **Market** | How big can it get? | A bottom-up sum: users you can reach × price | No credible figure for this category | A bottom-up size for the first group, then the wider one |
| **Competition** | Who else, and why do you win? | An honest map and one thing they cannot easily copy | 45 products mapped. Edge is a combination, not a wall | A one-page map, and the edge stated as a test passed |
| **Edge (moat)** | What gets stronger as you grow? | Data, habit, a network, a cost advantage | The memory of your projects grows with use. Unproven as a lock | Evidence that people who have a full memory leave less |
| **Business model** | How does money come in? | A price people already pay, and who pays | None tested | A price test with real users |
| **Unit economics** | Does each user earn more than they cost? | Cost to serve, cost to win, how long they stay | Cost to serve is known: about $10 a month for a heavy user | Margin at the tested price; the cost-cutting work (L16) |
| **Go-to-market** | How do the first 1,000 find you? | One channel that already works a little | None | One channel tried: a community, creators, or a launch |
| **Traction** | What has happened so far? | Use that grows, people who stay, money | One user | The numbers of steps 2 and 3 |
| **Team** | Why you? What is missing? | A reason this founder wins; a plan for the gaps | A designer-founder building with AI. No co-founder, no engineer | A plain page: what you bring, what you would hire first |
| **Plan and money** | What will you do with the money, by when? | 18 months, three milestones, a budget | None | A one-page plan and a simple budget |
| **Risks** | What kills this? | The real ones, each with what you do about it | Listed honestly in `01-foundation.md` | The top five, each with a test |
| **Legal and basics** | Is it clean? | A company, ownership of the code, a free name | No company; name not checked | Company, trademark and domain check, terms for users |

**The documents that carry this.** A pitch deck of 10 to 12 slides in this order: problem, product demo, why now, market, how it works, traction, business model, competition, go-to-market, team, the ask. A two-page memo for people who read. A short demo video. A "data room": one folder with the deck, the numbers, the plan and the legal papers.

## 14.5 What this changes in how we build now

Your instruction of 15 November: the MVP builds what is useful, not details.

- **Lean phases.** PH went from 14 tasks to 8, PA from 9 to 5, PB from 6 to 3. The separate hand-check phase (PT) is folded into the MVP test.
- **What is not cut, and why.** A short list keeps a product that acts on your Mac from doing harm: the things he never touches, Stop, and stopping on surprises. It is one task, not a phase. It is also what any user, and any investor's diligence, will ask about first.
- **Look and feel is now a phase in the MVP (PX).** For a product whose whole promise is "effortless", the experience is the product. The demo an investor sees is the UI.
- **The MVP test asks the CEO question.** PM.3: of 5 real jobs you ask him to do, are 4 done right?
- **After the MVP: L17 Venture readiness**, the right-hand column of 14.4, in the order of 14.3.

## 14.6 The three things I would decide next, as CEO

1. **Pick the first group** (14.2). It decides the demo, the interviews and the channel. Not needed to finish the MVP; needed the day after.
2. **Decide what step 2 costs you.** Giving it to 10 people needs a signed app and a key they do not have to create. That is the first work after the MVP, before any pitch.
3. **Decide what "success" is for you personally:** a company you raise for, a small paid tool, or a portfolio piece that opens doors. The path in 14.3 keeps all three open until step 3.
